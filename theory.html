⚖️ The Five Core Rules of MLFQ Scheduling

Rule 1: Priority Selection

If Priority(A) > Priority(B), process A executes. Processes situated in higher priority queues (Q0) are always scheduled before those in lower queues (Q1, Q2).

Rule 2: Round Robin within Priority Level

If Priority(A) = Priority(B), processes share CPU execution in Round Robin fashion using a time slice quantum of exactly 3 units.

Rule 3: Top-Queue Entry for New Arrivals

When any new process arrives into the system at random timestamp T, it is immediately enqueued into the highest priority queue (Q0).

Rule 4: Feedback Priority Demotion

Once a process uses up its allotted time quantum (3 units) in queue Q_i without completing its required CPU burst, its priority is reduced: it is demoted to Q_{i+1}.

Rule 5: Completion & Metrics Locking

When a process finishes its total required CPU burst time, it immediately transitions to the TERMINATED state and its Turnaround Time and Waiting Time are locked.

🔄 PCB Lifecycle & State Transitions
[NEW] Process Created at Random Arrival Time T │ ▼ [READY in Q0] Placed in Highest Priority Queue │ ▼ [RUNNING] Dispatched to CPU Core (Executes up to 3 units) ├── Completed (Rem = 0) ─────────► [TERMINATED] (Metrics Recorded) ├── Quantum Expired (3 units) ───► [READY in Q1] (Demoted) └── Preempted by Q0 Arrival ────► [READY in Qi] (Re-queued at head)
💡 Why UNIX MLFQ Approximates Shortest Job First (SJF)

A fundamental challenge in operating system design is that the operating system cannot know beforehand how long a newly submitted process will execute.

MLFQ solves this problem by first assuming every job is short and interactive, granting it top priority in Q0. If the job is indeed short (CPU burst ≤ 3 units), it completes immediately in Q0 with minimal waiting and turnaround time. If the job is actually a heavy batch task, it repeatedly consumes its time slices and gradually sinks into lower-priority queues (Q1 → Q2), allowing newly arriving interactive tasks to preempt it and receive prompt service.
